Midnight Train to Chihuahua (1913)

In November 1913, a cold wind was blowing across the northern desert of Mexico while hundreds of revolutionary soldiers were waiting beside the railway tracks. Young Elena Morales was carrying medical supplies and warm blankets for the wounded troops. Her older brother, Mateo, was inspecting the steam locomotive with General Villa’s scouts when a distant telegraph signal flashed in the dark.

Suddenly, the engine whistled, and the soldiers climbed onto the wooden boxcars. While the train was speeding toward Ciudad Juárez under the stars, Elena wrote letters for soldiers who could not read or write, and Mateo guarded the supplies. By sunrise, they reached the camp safely, proving that ordinary citizens and brave soldaderas kept the Revolution moving forward every single day.

Grammar Mastery: Simple Past & Past Continuous

Study the complete grammar explanation and examples below, then complete all 12 practice items.

1. Simple Past Tense

Used for completed actions at a specific time in the past, or short actions that interrupt an ongoing event.

  • Affirmative (+): Subject + Verb(-ed / irregular).
    "Madero wrote the Plan of San Luis in 1910."
  • Negative (-): Subject + did not (didn't) + base verb.
    "Porfirio Díaz did not listen to the farmers."
  • Interrogative (?): Did + subject + base verb?
    "Did Villa capture Torreón in 1913?"

2. Past Continuous Tense

Used for actions in progress at a particular moment in the past, or background scenes interrupted by a Simple Past event.

  • Affirmative (+): Subject + was / were + Verb-ing.
    "Soldiers were marching across Morelos."
  • Negative (-): Subject + was / were not + Verb-ing.
    "The factories were not paying fair wages."
  • Interrogative (?): Was / Were + subject + Verb-ing?
    "Were the delegates writing the constitution?"

3. Commands (Imperatives in Historical & Classroom Contexts)

Commands use the base form of the verb (or Do not / Don't + base verb) to give direct orders, instructions, or rallying cries.

• Affirmative Command: "Defend the land and protect the villages!"
• Negative Command: "Do not abandon the railway station!"

Audio9: The Mexican Revolution Narration

The Mexican Revolution

The Mexican Revolution began in 1910. At that time, Porfirio Díaz was the president of Mexico. He governed the country for more than thirty years. Some people were rich, but many people were poor. Farmers worked very hard on large farms, and they did not have their own land. Workers in factories earned little money, and they worked long hours. Many families wanted a better life, but the government did not listen to them. This made people angry.

Francisco Madero wanted a change. He said that Mexico needed free elections, and he asked the people to fight against Díaz. In November 1910, many Mexicans took up arms. Díaz left the country in 1911, and Madero became president.

Other leaders also became important. Emiliano Zapata fought in the south of Mexico. He wanted land for the farmers, and his famous words were “Land and Liberty.” Pancho Villa fought in the north. He and his soldiers traveled by train and won many battles. Venustiano Carranza was another leader, and he later became president.

The war was long and difficult. Many people lost their homes, and about one million people died. Women also played an important role. Some women cooked for the soldiers, some took care of the wounded, and some even fought in battles. They were called “soldaderas.” Life was very hard for everyone in those years.

In 1917, Mexico wrote a new constitution. This important law gave workers more rights and protected the land of the farmers. It also said that children could go to school for free. The fighting slowly ended in the 1920s.

Today, Mexicans remember the Revolution every year on November 20. People watch parades, and schools tell the stories of the heroes. The Revolution changed Mexico, and it is still an important part of the country’s history.
